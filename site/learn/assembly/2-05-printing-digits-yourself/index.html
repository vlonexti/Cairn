Printing Digits Yourself

lesson 2.055 of 5 in milestone 25 min1 practice task

To print a number, split it into decimal digits with div - by 100 for the hundreds, by 10 for the tens, the remainder for the units - and turn each digit into a character by adding '0'. Store the characters in a buffer and write them. print_int does the same with a loop, building the digits from the right.

Digits are remainders

The number 427 is 4 hundreds, 2 tens and 7 units. Division pulls them apart:

quotient remainder
427 ÷ 100 4 27
27 ÷ 10 2 7

Each digit 0 to 9 becomes its character by adding '0' (48): the characters '0' to '9' have the codes 48 to 57, in order. So 4 becomes '4' (52).

A three-digit printer

Assembly
        global  _start

        section .bss
digits: resb    4               ; three digits and a new line

        section .text
_start:
        mov     eax, 427        ; the number, 0 to 999

        xor     edx, edx
        mov     ecx, 100
        div     ecx             ; eax = hundreds, edx = the rest
        add     al, '0'
        mov     [rel digits], al

        mov     eax, edx        ; the rest, 0 to 99
        xor     edx, edx
        mov     ecx, 10
        div     ecx             ; eax = tens, edx = units
        add     al, '0'
        mov     [rel digits + 1], al
        add     dl, '0'
        mov     [rel digits + 2], dl
        mov     byte [rel digits + 3], 10

        mov     eax, 1          ; write the 4 bytes
        mov     edi, 1
        lea     rsi, [rel digits]
        mov     edx, 4
        syscall

        mov     eax, 60
        xor     edi, edi
        syscall
  • div ecx is the 32-bit form: it divides edx:eax by ecx. That is enough for small numbers and faster than the 64-bit form.
  • mov [rel digits + 1], al stores one byte at the address digits + 1. Lesson 4.01 covers addresses.
  • This version always prints three digits: 7 comes out as 007.

Any number of digits

A number can have up to 20 decimal digits (2⁶⁴ is about 1.8 × 10¹⁹). Instead of a fixed set of divisions, print_uint repeats one step: divide by 10, and the remainder is the last digit. Then the quotient's last digit is the one before, and so on, until the quotient is 0. Because the digits come out from right to left, it fills its buffer backwards:

Assembly
print_uint:
        sub     rsp, 40                 ; room on the stack for the digits (lesson 4.04)
        lea     rsi, [rsp + 32]         ; start at the right-hand end
        mov     rax, rdi
        mov     ecx, 10
.digit: xor     edx, edx
        div     rcx                     ; rax = rax / 10, rdx = the last digit
        add     dl, '0'
        dec     rsi                     ; move one place left
        mov     [rsi], dl               ; store the character
        test    rax, rax
        jnz     .digit                  ; more digits while the quotient is not 0
        ...                             ; then write from rsi to the end

jnz means "jump if not zero", back to .digit: a loop. That is the subject of module 3. After it, you will write your own version, and lesson 6.02 handles signed numbers and reading digits back in.

Printing in other bases

The same method works for any base: divide by 2 for binary, by 16 for hexadecimal (where digits 10 to 15 become 'a' to 'f'). Since 2 and 16 are powers of two, shifts and masks replace the divisions: the last hex digit is n & 15, and n >> 4 drops it. That is how print_hex works.
