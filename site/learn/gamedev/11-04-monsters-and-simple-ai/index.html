Monsters and Simple AI

lesson 11.044 of 8 in milestone 115 min

Delve's monsters need to notice the player, chase them around walls, and attack when adjacent. This lesson gives each monster a small state machine, and replaces per-monster pathfinding with one distance map (a breadth-first search from the player, lesson 3.09) that every monster follows downhill. It is simple, fast, and the basis of AI in many roguelikes.

A state machine per monster

Each monster is in one of a few states, and simple rules move it between them:

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Idle
    Idle --> Chasing: sees or hears the player
    Chasing --> Attacking: next to the player
    Attacking --> Chasing: player moved away
    Chasing --> Idle: lost the player for 10 turns
    Attacking --> [*]: killed
    Chasing --> [*]: killed
State Each turn
Idle Wander randomly, or stand still; check whether the player is within sight range
Chasing Step one tile towards the player
Attacking Hit the player (lesson 11.05)

States are just an enum in a component (lesson 10.03) and a switch in the AI system: small, readable and easy to debug, which matters more than sophistication for most game AI.

Chasing: one search for all monsters

Each chasing monster needs the next step towards the player around the walls. Running a separate BFS from every monster to the player works, but it repeats almost the same work for each one. Instead, run one BFS starting from the player, recording every reachable tile's distance (in steps) to the player:

Output
#########         #########
#.......#         #8765456#
#.###...#    →    #9###345#
#.#@....#         #A#@1234#
#########         #########
  map               distance map (A is 10; walls have none)

Now every monster, wherever it is, finds its next step by looking at its four neighbours and moving to the one with the smallest distance: it walks "downhill" to the player. One search per turn serves any number of monsters, and each monster's decision is just four lookups. This structure is called a distance map (or "Dijkstra map", or flow field). The practice task builds it.

Useful variations come almost for free:

  • Fleeing: a monster with low health moves to a neighbour with a larger distance, away from the player.
  • Several goals: start the search from every item and the monster heads to the nearest one.
  • Terrain costs: treat mud as costing 2 steps (Dijkstra's algorithm instead of BFS) and monsters route around it.

Taking turns fairly

After the player acts, every monster acts once, in a fixed order (for example by entity id). A few details make it robust:

  • Recompute the distance map once after the player moves, before any monster acts.
  • Two monsters may want the same tile. Process them in order and let the second one choose again (or wait) if the tile is now taken.
  • Monsters that are far away and idle can skip the expensive parts entirely.

Seeing the player

"Sees the player" can start simple: within 8 tiles by distance-map steps (which already accounts for walls, so monsters do not notice the player through a wall). A proper line-of-sight check traces a line across the grid (Bresenham's line algorithm) and stops at the first wall; it is a good extension once the basics work.

Common mistakes

Running a pathfinding search per monster per turn

One distance map from the player serves them all.

Letting monsters move onto each other

Check occupancy as each monster moves, not only before the turn starts.

AI that is too clever

Players enjoy monsters that are readable and predictable. Simple rules, clearly communicated, beat complex ones.
