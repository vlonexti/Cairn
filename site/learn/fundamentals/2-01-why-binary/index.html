Why Binary

lesson 2.011 of 6 in milestone 25 min1 practice task

Computers use binary - only 0 and 1 - because a switch that is either on or off is cheap, tiny, fast and very hard to misread. Ten different voltage levels would be easy to confuse; two are not. Every kind of information can be built from enough 0s and 1s.

Two states are easy to tell apart

Inside a chip, a bit is a voltage on a tiny wire. A high voltage (say, about 1 volt) means 1; close to 0 volts means 0.

Why not use ten voltage levels and store a whole decimal digit on each wire? Because real electronics are noisy. Wires pick up interference, temperature shifts things, transistors are never exactly identical. With ten levels squeezed into one volt, each level is only a tenth of a volt from its neighbours, and a little noise turns a 6 into a 7. With two levels, the gap is huge: anything near the top is 1, anything near the bottom is 0, and noise would have to be enormous to flip it.

Reason Why it matters
Reliable Two states far apart are almost impossible to confuse
Simple A transistor is naturally a switch: on or off
Cheap and small Simple parts can be made tiny, so billions fit on a chip
Fits logic True and false, and Boole's AND, OR and NOT, map directly onto 1 and 0 (lesson 4.01)
Not every computer was binary

ENIAC (1.03) stored decimal digits, and the Soviet Setun of 1958 used three states. Binary won because it is the most reliable and cheapest to build.

One bit is not much; many bits are everything

One bit answers one yes-or-no question: on or off, true or false, heads or tails. That is not much. But bits combine:

Bits Different patterns Enough for
1 2 yes / no
2 4 the four seasons
3 8 the days of the week (with one spare)
8 256 every key on a keyboard, with room to spare
16 65,536 every value a 16-bit number can hold
24 about 16.7 million every colour a screen can show
32 about 4.3 billion a number for every person on Earth, nearly

Each extra bit doubles the number of patterns, because every old pattern can now be followed by a 0 or by a 1. With n bits there are 2 × 2 × ... × 2 (n times) = 2ⁿ patterns.

Meaning is up to us

A pattern of bits has no meaning by itself. 01000001 could be:

  • the number 65;
  • the letter A (lesson 3.01);
  • part of a colour, a sound, or a machine instruction.

What a pattern means depends entirely on how the program uses it. A computer does not know it is holding a letter; a program decides to treat those bits as one. This idea - the same bits, different meanings - comes back in every milestone of this course.
