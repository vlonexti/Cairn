Colours, Images and Sound

lesson 3.044 of 5 in milestone 35 min1 practice task

A colour is three numbers - red, green and blue light, usually 0 to 255 each. An image is a grid of those colours, and compression makes it smaller. Sound is the air pressure measured tens of thousands of times a second, each measurement a number.

Colour: three numbers

A screen makes every colour by mixing three lights: red, green and blue (RGB). Each is a number, usually from 0 to 255 (one byte):

Colour Red Green Blue Hex
Black 0 0 0 #000000
White 255 255 255 #FFFFFF
Red 255 0 0 #FF0000
Yellow (red + green light) 255 255 0 #FFFF00
Orange 255 136 0 #FF8800
Mid grey 128 128 128 #808080

Three bytes per colour is 24 bits: 256 × 256 × 256 = 16,777,216 colours. The hex form you see in web pages and design tools is just the three bytes in a row: #FF8800 is FF (255) red, 88 (136) green, 00 blue.

Light mixes differently from paint: red and green light make yellow, and all three at full make white. Often there is a fourth byte, alpha, for how see-through a pixel is: RGBA, 32 bits per pixel.

Images: a grid of colours

An image is a grid of pixels, each one colour. Size is easy to work out:

1920 × 1080 pixels × 3 bytes = 6,220,800 bytes, about 6 MB, for one uncompressed full-HD picture.

A phone photo of 12 million pixels would be 36 MB that way, yet the file is about 3 MB. That is compression:

Kind How Examples Good for
Lossless Finds repetition and stores it more briefly ("200 white pixels" instead of 200 copies). Decompresses to exactly the original PNG, GIF, ZIP Screenshots, diagrams, text, anything with sharp edges
Lossy Throws away detail the eye barely notices JPEG, WebP, and video: H.264, AV1 Photos and video, where files become 10 to 100 times smaller

A video is a series of images, typically 24 to 60 per second, compressed by also storing mostly what changes between frames.

Sound: measuring the air

Sound is the air pressure wobbling back and forth. A microphone turns it into a changing voltage, and the computer samples it: measures it many times a second and stores each measurement as a number.

flowchart LR
    A["Air pressure wave"] --> M["Microphone<br/>voltage"] --> C["Converter samples it<br/>44,100 times a second"] --> N["Numbers:<br/>0, 2034, 3871, 5102, ..."]
Setting CD audio Meaning
Sample rate 44,100 per second Must be over twice the highest pitch people hear (about 20,000 Hz)
Bits per sample 16 Each measurement is a number from −32,768 to 32,767
Channels 2 Left and right

One second of CD audio: 44,100 × 2 bytes × 2 channels = 176,400 bytes. A 3-minute song is about 32 MB uncompressed, and about 3 MB as MP3 or AAC, which are lossy formats that drop sounds people cannot hear well.

To play sound, the computer sends the numbers back to a converter that turns them into a voltage, which moves a speaker, which moves the air.

Everything is numbers

Text (3.01), fractions (3.03), colours, pictures, sound: each is turned into numbers by an agreed rule, and the numbers into bits. Whatever a computer seems to work with, underneath it is working with numbers, and the program is what remembers what they mean.
