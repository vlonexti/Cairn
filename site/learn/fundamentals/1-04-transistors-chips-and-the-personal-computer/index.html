Transistors, Chips and the Personal Computer

lesson 1.044 of 5 in milestone 15 min1 practice task

The transistor replaced the vacuum tube, the integrated circuit put many transistors on one chip, and the microprocessor put a whole CPU on one chip. That made computers small and cheap enough for homes - the Altair, the Apple II, the IBM PC - and Moore's law kept them doubling.

The transistor (1947)

At Bell Labs in 1947, John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley built the first transistor: a switch made from a crystal of semiconductor (first germanium, later silicon). It did the job of a vacuum tube, but:

  • it had no hot filament to burn out;
  • it used a tiny fraction of the power;
  • it could be made very, very small.

A transistor has three connections. A small voltage on the middle one (the gate) decides whether current can flow between the other two. On or off: one bit, switched by electricity.

The integrated circuit (1958-59)

Early transistor computers were built from thousands of separate transistors soldered together by hand. In 1958 Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) and in 1959 Robert Noyce (Fairchild) made integrated circuits: many transistors, and the wires between them, made together on one small piece of silicon. A chip.

Chips are made by printing patterns onto silicon with light, layer by layer, a bit like photography. Once the process works, making a million chips costs little more per chip than making a thousand, and every improvement in the printing makes the transistors smaller.

The microprocessor (1971)

In 1971 Intel released the 4004: a whole CPU on one chip. It had about 2,300 transistors, worked on 4 bits at a time, and ran at 740 thousand steps a second. It was designed for a calculator.

Year Chip Bits Transistors Used in
1971 Intel 4004 4 2,300 Calculators
1974 Intel 8080 8 6,000 The Altair 8800
1975 MOS 6502 8 3,500 Apple II, Commodore 64, the NES
1978 Intel 8086 16 29,000 The start of x86: the family PCs still use
1985 Intel 80386 32 275,000 32-bit PCs and Windows
2003 AMD Athlon 64 64 about 100 million x86-64: today's PCs
2020 Apple M1 64 16 billion Macs, using ARM instead of x86

The "bits" column matters: it is how big a number the chip handles in one step, and how much memory it can point at. Lesson 2.06 explains it properly.

The personal computer

With a CPU on one cheap chip, a computer could fit on a desk:

Year Machine Why it matters
1975 Altair 8800 A kit you built yourself, programmed with switches on the front. Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote a BASIC for it and founded Microsoft
1977 Apple II Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs: a ready-made computer with a keyboard, colour graphics and games
1981 IBM PC IBM's business computer, using Intel's 8088 and Microsoft's MS-DOS. Other companies copied it, and "PC compatible" became the standard
1984 Apple Macintosh Made the mouse, windows and icons mainstream (ideas from Xerox PARC)

Moore's law

In 1965 Gordon Moore (later a founder of Intel) noticed that the number of transistors on a chip was doubling regularly. In 1975 he settled on roughly every two years. This "law" was really a prediction and a target the industry chased, and it held for about fifty years:

Year Transistors on a top chip
1971 2,300
1991 about 1 million
2011 about 2 billion
2024 over 100 billion

Doubling every two years for fifty years is a factor of about 30 million. That is why a phone outruns a 1990s supercomputer. Transistors are now only a few dozen atoms wide, so the doubling has slowed; chips get faster now mostly by having more cores and special parts for graphics and AI (lesson 1.05).
