Counting Machines and Looms

lesson 1.022 of 5 in milestone 16 min1 practice task

Before electricity, people built machines to count and calculate - the abacus, Pascal's adding machine, Babbage's engines - and a loom that read patterns from punched cards. Ada Lovelace wrote the first published computer program for a machine that was never finished.

Counting with beads

People have always needed to count: sheep, grain, money, days. The oldest calculating tool still in use is the abacus, beads on rods, used for thousands of years from Mesopotamia to China and Rome. Each rod is one place: units, tens, hundreds. Moving beads is adding.

The abacus does not calculate by itself - the person moving the beads does. But it already shows the most important idea in this course: a number can be stored as the position of physical things. Beads up or down. Later, gears turned. Today, switches on or off.

Machines that add: Pascal and Leibniz

Year Machine What it could do
1642 Pascaline, by Blaise Pascal (aged 19, to help his father with tax sums) Add and subtract with turning gear wheels. When a wheel passed 9, it pushed the next wheel on by one: the carry, done by machinery
1673 Stepped reckoner, by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz Add, subtract, multiply and divide

Leibniz did something else that matters to us: in 1703 he published a description of binary, counting with only the digits 0 and 1. He thought it was beautiful; he had no idea that, 250 years later, every computer would use it.

The carry in the Pascaline is exactly what a computer's adder does with bits today (lesson 4.02). The problem is the same; only the parts changed.

A loom that reads cards

In 1804 Joseph-Marie Jacquard built a loom for weaving patterned silk that was controlled by a chain of punched cards. Each card had rows of holes; a hole let a hook through and lifted a thread, no hole kept it down. Change the cards, and the same loom wove a different pattern.

That is a machine following a program stored outside the machine. The cards were the software; the loom was the hardware. A hole or no hole is one bit.

Punched cards stayed with computing for a long time: programs were still being typed onto punched cards in the 1970s.

Babbage's engines

Charles Babbage, an English mathematician, was annoyed by errors in printed tables of numbers (used by sailors, engineers and banks), all worked out by hand. He designed two machines:

  • The Difference Engine (designed from 1822): a machine to work out and print tables of numbers using only addition, by a method called finite differences. It was not finished in his lifetime. A working one was built from his drawings by the Science Museum in London in 1991, and it works.
  • The Analytical Engine (designed from 1837): far more ambitious, and the first design for a general-purpose computer. It had:
Babbage's name What we call it today
The mill The processor: where the arithmetic happens
The store Memory: 1,000 numbers of 40 decimal digits
Punched cards (borrowed from Jacquard) The program, and the input
A printer Output

It could repeat a set of instructions (a loop) and choose what to do next based on a result (a condition). It was never built - the engineering of the time could not make the thousands of precise parts - but the design was a real computer, a hundred years early. The Difference Engine is the subject of this milestone's project.

Ada Lovelace, the first programmer

Ada Lovelace was a mathematician who worked with Babbage. In 1843 she translated an article about the Analytical Engine and added her own notes, three times as long as the article. Note G contains a step-by-step method for the engine to compute Bernoulli numbers: the first published computer program.

She also saw something Babbage did not stress: the machine worked on symbols, not only numbers. If music or letters could be written as numbers, she wrote, the engine could work on them too. That is exactly what computers do today (milestone 3).

A programming language named after her

The language Ada, created in 1980 for the US Department of Defense and still used for aircraft and railway software, is named after her.

Boole's logic and Hollerith's cards

Two more pieces arrived in the 1800s:

  • George Boole (1854) wrote down the rules of logic as algebra: true and false, combined with AND, OR and NOT. It looked like pure philosophy. In 1937 Claude Shannon showed that electrical switch circuits follow exactly Boole's rules, which is why it is the maths of every computer chip (lesson 4.01).
  • Herman Hollerith built electric tabulating machines that read punched cards to count the 1890 US census. A job that had taken eight years by hand took about one. His company later became IBM.
