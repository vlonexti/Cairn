Computers Today

lesson 1.055 of 5 in milestone 15 min

Today's computers - laptops, phones, game consoles, servers - are still von Neumann machines, but with many cores, graphics processors, layers of fast memory, and chips designed around low power. The two main families are x86 (most PCs) and ARM (phones and new Macs).

The same ideas, everywhere

There are far more computers around you than the ones called computers: a phone, a watch, a car (often over 50 small computers), a washing machine, a games console, a router. Inside every one is the same thing you met in 1.03: a CPU fetching instructions from memory and carrying them out.

Two families: x86 and ARM

A CPU understands one particular set of instructions, its instruction set architecture (ISA). A program built for one ISA does not run on another, the way a book in French cannot be read by someone who only reads English.

Family Who makes it Where it is Style
x86-64 (also called x64, AMD64) Intel, AMD Most Windows PCs, most servers, PlayStation 5, Xbox Many complex instructions; descends from the 8086 of 1978
ARM64 (also called AArch64) Designed by Arm, built by Apple, Qualcomm, Samsung and others Almost every phone and tablet, Apple-chip Macs, Nintendo Switch, many servers Fewer, simpler instructions; built around low power

This is why an app is built separately for each: the same program is translated into x86-64 machine code for one, and ARM64 machine code for the other. The Assembly course on this site uses x86-64.

Many cores

Around 2005, making one CPU faster and faster ran into a wall: faster switching made chips too hot. The answer was to put several CPUs on one chip. Each is called a core.

A laptop typically has 4 to 16 cores; a big server, over a hundred. Four cores can do four things at once - but only if the program is written to split its work up (lesson 6.02). A program that does one thing after another uses one core and leaves the rest idle.

GPUs

A GPU (graphics processing unit) is a chip with thousands of small, simple cores, all doing the same kind of sum at the same time on different data. That is exactly what drawing a picture needs: work out the colour of each of 8 million pixels, 60 or more times a second.

The same design is perfect for the maths of AI, which is why modern AI is trained on GPUs, and why phones and new laptops now also have an NPU (neural processing unit) for AI tasks.

Speed in numbers

Thing Typical today
Clock speed of one core 3 to 5 GHz (billion ticks a second)
Cores in a laptop 4 to 16
RAM 8 to 64 GB
Storage (SSD) 256 GB to 4 TB
A phone's chip 10 to 20 billion transistors

You will learn what GB, GHz and the other units mean exactly in milestone 2.

Where computers live now

  • Personal devices: phones, laptops, tablets, consoles.
  • Embedded computers: small chips inside other things - cars, cameras, microwaves - often with no screen at all, running one program forever.
  • The cloud: huge buildings of servers (data centres). When you stream a film or ask a chatbot something, a computer in a data centre does much of the work and sends you the result over the internet (lesson 6.06).
